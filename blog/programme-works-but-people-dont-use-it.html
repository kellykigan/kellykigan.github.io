What if the programme works, but people don't use it?

27 April 2026 | 3 min read | By Kelly Kigan

A service can be well designed. The funding can be there. The infrastructure can be built. And people may still not use it.

Maybe it costs too much. Maybe it is too far away. Maybe people do not trust it. Maybe it does not fit how they live.

Implementation data tells you whether the service exists. It does not tell you whether anyone uses it. A programme can work perfectly on paper and still make no difference to people's lives. At that point the question is the one every programme manager dreads: why aren't people using it?

Price changes who shows up

In western Kenya, Jessica Cohen and Pascaline Dupas randomised the price at which prenatal clinics sold insecticide-treated bed nets. Demand was 75 percent lower at the $0.75 cost-sharing price than when nets were free. Women who paid were no more likely to use the net than women who received it free (J-PAL summary of Cohen and Dupas, 2010).

Charging a small fee did not screen for people who would use the net. It removed people who would have used it.

Reliability and small costs matter

In rural Rajasthan, India, immunisation was free at public facilities, yet few children completed the schedule. Researchers tested monthly immunisation camps that ran on a reliable schedule. Full immunisation among children aged 1 to 3 rose from 6 percent in comparison villages to 18 percent with reliable camps. It reached 39 percent when parents also received small incentives of lentils and metal plates (Banerjee et al., 2010).

The parents were not hostile to vaccines. Even without incentives, 78 percent of children had received at least one vaccine. The drop came later in the schedule, where the time and transport costs of repeat clinic visits can deter people from finishing (Bates and Glennerster, 2017).

That case shows that uptake has stages. A programme can look successful at first contact and fail at completion.

Measure uptake as well as delivery

Administrative records count the people who showed up. They miss the people who did not. Four habits close the gap:

  • Define the eligible population first. Then measure the share of that population who use the service.
  • Separate the stages. Awareness, first use, repeat use and completion are different outcomes with different causes.
  • Break results down. Distance, sex, income and location often explain who is missing. National and district averages hide these gaps (see how averages mislead).
  • Ask the people who did not use the service. Short interviews and focus group discussions give reasons that a survey of users cannot.

When uptake is low, diagnose before redesigning

The fixes differ by cause. If price is the barrier, a lower price or free provision raises demand, as the Kenyan bed net trial showed. If unreliable supply is the barrier, reliability comes first, as in the Rajasthan camps. If the barrier is travel and time, bringing the service closer or bundling visits may matter more than any incentive.

Guessing wrong is expensive. A programme that adds incentives when the real problem is a clinic that opens irregularly will spend money and see little change. Ask non-users first, pilot the fix on a small scale, and measure uptake again at each stage before scaling.

Questions to ask before launch

Run through the four doubts from the start of this article as design questions. What does the service cost a user, once travel and lost working time are included? Is it open when people can actually come? Do people trust the provider, and how would you find out? Does the design fit daily routines?

Trust is the hardest of these to measure. Ask about it directly, in the community's own language, and listen for specifics such as past experiences with a clinic or office.

When a programme works on paper, the evaluation question changes. It is no longer "was it delivered?" It becomes "why aren't people using it?"
