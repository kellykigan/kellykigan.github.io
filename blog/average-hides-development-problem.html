The average can hide the development problem

21 September 2026 | 3 min read | By Kelly Kigan

A national average can look like good news while a specific group gets nothing from it.

Kenya's child stunting data shows how. The 2022 Kenya Demographic and Health Survey found that stunting among children under five fell from 26 percent in 2014 to 18 percent in 2022 (Nutrition International). The Kenya National Bureau of Statistics reports the same 18 percent figure (KNBS). By any national measure, that is progress.

Behind the 18 percent

County figures tell a different story. Kilifi recorded 37 percent stunting, West Pokot 34 percent and Samburu 31 percent, against 18 percent nationally (The Standard). Children in Kilifi are about twice as likely to be stunted as the national figure implies, and West Pokot and Samburu are not far behind.

Wealth shows the same split. About 28 percent of children under five in the lowest wealth quintile were stunted (GAIN, citing KDHS 2022). A national figure has no way to show that gap. It reports where most children happen to live.

Averages also weight places by population. A large county with low stunting pulls the national figure down and makes smaller, harder-hit counties disappear from the headline.

The same pattern in human development

The UNDP's inequality-adjusted Human Development Index applies the same idea to a whole country. In UNDP's 2019 report, Kenya lost 26.3 percent of its human development value to inequality in the distribution of health, education and income. The average loss for sub-Saharan Africa was 30.5 percent (UNDP). These figures are several years old, but they illustrate the method: discount the average for how unevenly it is shared.

How an average is built

The measure you pick matters too. Take five households with monthly incomes of 10,000, 12,000, 14,000, 15,000 and 200,000 shillings. This example is hypothetical. The mean is 50,200 shillings and the median is 14,000. The mean describes none of the five households. The median describes the typical one. Income and wealth data are skewed in most countries, so a mean often overstates what a typical household has.

What to do about it

  • Disaggregate by default. Report results by county, sex, wealth group and rural or urban location. If the data cannot be broken down, say so.
  • Report distributions as well as means. Use medians, percentiles and the gap between the best-off and worst-off groups. One high earner moves a mean. It barely moves a median.
  • Set targets for the gap as well as the average. A programme that lifts the national figure by leaving the poorest counties untouched has met a target and missed the problem.
  • Check whether the survey can support the breakdown. The KDHS was designed to give estimates at national and urban or rural level and, for some indicators, at county level (Nutrition International). County estimates carry wider margins of error than national ones, so read them with confidence intervals.
  • Target resources with the breakdown. A nutrition budget allocated from the national 18 percent would under-serve Kilifi.

What the headline number is still good for

None of this makes national averages useless. They track direction over time and allow comparison across countries, and a fall from 26 to 18 percent is real progress. The problem starts when the average is the only number reported. It should open the discussion, not close it.

Where I apply this

In my gender development project, I compare the Gender Development Index across human development groups and regions instead of relying on a single global figure. The project reports the differences between groups instead of one mean.

When someone presents a headline number, a good question is "for whom?" It takes five seconds to ask and often changes the decision. Uptake data raises the same question: who is missing from the people a programme reaches?

An average describes the middle. Development problems often sit at the edges.
