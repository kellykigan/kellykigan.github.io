When the data says one thing and the community says another

12 October 2026 | 3 min read | By Kelly Kigan

The survey says 90 percent of households live within five kilometres of a health clinic. The community says the clinic is out of reach.

Both can be right. This example is hypothetical, but the pattern is common in development work. The survey measured distance. The community describes access: a river that floods in the rainy season, a nurse who is absent on weekends, a queue that starts at dawn. Data answers the question it was asked. Communities answer the question they live with.

Treat disagreement as a finding

When numbers and community accounts conflict, the usual instinct is to pick a winner. A better instinct is to treat the conflict as a signal about measurement or context. One side did not lie. They measured different things.

The World Bank's Voices of the Poor study reflects this idea at scale. For the World Development Report 2000/2001, it brought together the experiences of more than 60,000 poor women and men from 60 countries, using open-ended qualitative and participatory methods. The aim was to illuminate the human experience behind poverty statistics (Narayan, 2000).

Why mixed methods exist

Michael Bamberger, Vijayendra Rao and Michael Woolcock describe how qualitative methods can address limits of randomised trials and other quantitative impact evaluation methods. They also help distinguish design failures from implementation failures (Bamberger, Rao and Woolcock, 2010).

That distinction matters when data and communities disagree. A survey indicator that looks healthy can hide a programme that is badly implemented. Community accounts often point to the implementation detail that a standard indicator cannot capture.

Consultation as evidence

Some standards make community voice a requirement. The IFC's Performance Standard 7 on Indigenous Peoples treats informed consultation and participation throughout the project process as a core requirement, and it may include free, prior and informed consent in some circumstances (IFC).

I have analysed consultation records gathered under Performance Standard 7 for a donor-funded climate resilience programme in Papua New Guinea, using thematic analysis to assess community engagement outcomes. Consultation records are data. They deserve the same rigour as a household survey: clear coding, a record of who spoke, and a record of who did not.

A way to reconcile the two

  • Check what the indicator measured. Who answered, when, and with what wording? Distance to a clinic and ability to use it are different variables.
  • Take the numbers back to the community. Share preliminary results in a validation meeting and ask what is missing. Record the reactions as data.
  • Add a third source. Administrative records, direct observation or a short follow-up survey can show which account holds in practice.
  • Report both accounts and the gap. Do not average them. State what is established, what is disputed and what you could not resolve.
  • Change the instrument. If the survey missed something the community raised, add a question in the next round.

Document the disagreement

Keep a short log of each conflict between data and community accounts. Record what each source said, how each was collected, which checks you ran and what remains unresolved. The log shows later readers why a decision was made. It also stops the same disagreement from resurfacing in the next evaluation round as if it were new.

What changes for decision-makers

A programme manager who sees only the survey might conclude that access is solved. One who hears only the community might conclude that the whole system has failed. Reading both leads to a narrower and more useful decision: fix weekend staffing and the flood-prone road, and keep the clinics.

The same logic applies to low uptake of a programme that works on paper and to findings that do not transfer across settings. In each case, local knowledge explains what the headline number cannot.

When the data says one thing and the community says another, the disagreement is a finding. The work is to explain it.
