Evidence-based policy has a problem we don't talk about enough

6 April 2026 | 3 min read | By Kelly Kigan

We say we want evidence-based policy. We rarely ask whose evidence it is, or where it came from.

A study run in one district, with one implementing partner, under close supervision, gets cited as proof that a program works. The program then scales to places with thinner staffing and tighter budgets. The results often shrink or disappear. This is the external validity problem: whether a finding from one setting holds in another.

Same contract, different result in Kenya

Kenya offers a clean test. An earlier study found that contract teachers managed by an NGO raised test scores in Western Kenya by 0.21 standard deviations (Bold et al., 2013). In 2009 the government announced a national contract teacher program that would eventually employ 18,000 teachers.

Researchers embedded a randomised trial in that scale-up. Some schools received teachers hired by the NGO World Vision Kenya. Others received teachers hired by the government on the same terms (J-PAL summary). The NGO teachers significantly raised student test scores. The government teachers produced zero impact (Bold et al., 2018).

Differences in teacher characteristics explained little of the gap. The authors point to bureaucratic and political opposition to the reform, which delayed implementation and led to a different reading of identical contract terms. The teachers' union, for example, demanded permanent civil service status for contract teachers (J-PAL).

The lesson is not that contract teachers fail. A result depends on who delivers the program and under what conditions.

The pattern is wider than one program

Eva Vivalt assembled a large dataset of impact evaluation results and found a large amount of effect heterogeneity. Government-implemented programs had smaller effect sizes than programs run by academics or NGOs, even after controlling for sample size (Vivalt, 2020).

That finding matters because ministries, not research teams, run most public programs. An evidence base built mainly on pilots can overstate what a stretched district office will deliver.

Timing widens the gap

Budgets are set on an annual cycle. A rigorous evaluation that follows households over several years can report after the money has been allocated. Decision-makers then use whatever evidence is at hand, including studies from settings that look nothing like theirs.

This is not an argument against evaluation

Bates and Glennerster make the opposite case. Rigorous evaluations, they argue, complement local data rather than replace it, and the real task is to pair evidence from elsewhere with information from the place you are working in (Bates and Glennerster, 2017). A trial from another country tells you about behaviour. Local data tells you whether the conditions for that behaviour exist. Policy needs both.

Four fixes that cost less than a new trial

  • Fund replication. The 3ie Replication Programme has funded more than 20 internal replications since 2012, which re-check whether published results hold up (3ie). Internal replication tests an analysis. Testing a program in a new setting needs its own funding line.
  • Report implementation conditions next to effect sizes. Say who delivered the program, how many staff served each beneficiary, how closely they were supervised and what it cost per person. Vivalt's finding on implementers shows why readers need these facts.
  • Ask "will this work here?" before "does this work?" Mary Ann Bates and Rachel Glennerster of J-PAL propose four questions. What theory sits behind the program? Do the local conditions that theory needs hold? How strong is the evidence for the behaviour change it relies on? Can implementation be done well? (Bates and Glennerster, 2017)
  • Collect cheap local data before copying a program. Rwanda's government asked whether a Kenyan HIV-risk awareness program would work at home. Descriptive survey data collected over two weeks showed that Rwandan girls already understood the relative risk, so J-PAL did not recommend the program (Bates and Glennerster, 2017).

The next time a report calls a program proven, ask where it was tested and who delivered it.
