What learning R taught me about quantitative research

10 August 2026 | 3 min read | By Kelly Kigan

I have been spending more time in R lately, working through quantitative analysis for my development research. I picked it up to sharpen a technical skill. What I learned is that good quantitative work happens before you run a test.

Two projects shaped these notes: an analysis of UNESCO education indicators across African countries and a study of the Gender Development Index across 195 countries. A free resource I recommend for learning R is R for Data Science.

1. Understand the dataset before any test

Before touching a statistical test, I had to understand what the dataset contained. What did each variable measure? How was it coded? Which units did it use? Where were the odd values hiding?

For the education data, I read the indicator definitions before opening a script, because attendance, enrolment and school life expectancy measure different things. The gender development data covers 195 countries and 39 variables. I ran data quality and missing-data checks before any comparison. That groundwork decides whether anything downstream makes sense. I wrote more about this habit in your dataset has a story before you analyse it.

2. Cleaning is research

Cleaning the data was not a side task. Missing values, duplicates and inconsistent coding shape the conclusions you end up drawing. Fixing them is research, not preparation for research.

Every cleaning decision is a choice someone could challenge. In R, those choices sit in a script where others can read them.

3. Let the question pick the method

I also had to resist the urge to ask what I could run on this dataset. The better question was what I was trying to answer and what evidence would answer it.

Sometimes that means simple descriptive statistics. Sometimes it means regression. For the education project, the question came first: does spending on primary education go with attendance once time and location are accounted for? The method followed from that. Ideally the question picks the method, not the other way round. I expand on this in the best statistical tool is the one that fits the question.

4. Draw the data early

Visualising the data early helped me see distributions and relationships that tables could not show. It was a way to ask sharper questions before committing to an analysis.

Francis Anscombe made the same point in 1973 with four datasets that share the same means, variances and regression line but look nothing alike on a chart (Anscombe, 1973). A histogram takes seconds to draw.

5. Say what the data cannot tell you

The biggest lesson was that correlation is not causation. Development outcomes are shaped by overlapping economic, social and institutional factors. A regression coefficient cannot separate them on its own.

That is why the gender development write-up states that the data are observational, so the results show associations, not causal effects. Being honest about what the data cannot tell you matters as much as what it can. I explore the difference in correlation is easy, explaining why is hard.

What I would tell someone starting out

  • Begin with a question you care about. A dataset without a question produces charts without meaning.
  • Pick one public dataset and finish the project. UNESCO and UNDP data are free, and finishing one project teaches more than starting five.
  • Put the script on GitHub. Public code makes you write more clearly, and it lets others rerun your work.
  • Write the limits into the README. A reader who knows what a project cannot show can trust what it does show.

Both of my projects are public on GitHub with their data and code, so anyone can rerun them and check the numbers.
