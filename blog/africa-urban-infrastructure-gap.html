Where should Africa's urban infrastructure money go first?

8 June 2026 | 3 min read | By Kelly Kigan

Africa's cities are growing faster than the housing, roads, water systems and services built to support them.

Kigali grew from about 219,000 people in 1990 to 1.2 million in 2010, more than five-fold (Jones, Ferreira and Murray, 2016). Lilongwe's census count rose from 223,318 in 1987 to 989,318 in 2018 (National Statistical Office of Malawi, via citypopulation.de). The African Development Bank expects the number of people living in African cities to nearly triple, to about 1.5 billion by 2050 (AfDB).

The size of the gap

The African Development Bank estimates that Africa's infrastructure needs run to $130 billion to $170 billion a year, with a financing gap of $68 billion to $108 billion (AfDB, 2018). Its report on city finance says closing the gap requires more than doubling current investment, with the bulk deployed in cities (AfDB).

Treat quoted figures with care, because estimates differ by scope and method. The Africa Infrastructure Country Diagnostic, cited in the World Bank's Africa's Cities study, estimated annual needs of $68 billion to $93 billion (World Bank). The $150 billion figure that circulates comes from remarks about the continent's total infrastructure needs at the 2025 Africa Investment Forum, and it describes all infrastructure, not urban infrastructure alone (AfDB).

Where the gap shows

The gap shows up in informal settlements. In 2022, 53.6 percent of the urban population in sub-Saharan Africa lived in slums or slum-like conditions, the highest share of any region (UN Sustainable Development Goal 11). The same source projects the greatest proportional increase in slum dwellers in sub-Saharan Africa by 2030.

What the estimates do not tell you

The estimates describe scale. They do not rank investments by return, and they do not say which city should go first. Sequencing needs local data: where growth is heading, what land costs, how many households a given connection would reach and who pays for upkeep. National totals cannot answer those questions. City governments, working with donors, would need to collect the data.

The evidence base for sequencing is also thinner than the evidence for scale. I treat any ordered list, including the one below, as a hypothesis to test city by city.

Where should the money go first?

The World Bank's Africa's Cities study argues that African cities produce few goods and services for trade. Part of the reason is that they lack functioning land markets and regulations, and early, coordinated infrastructure investment (World Bank). That evidence points to an order of priorities. What follows is my reading of it.

  • Land and network backbone ahead of settlement. Roads, water mains and drainage are easier to build before households settle along the route. They also set the pattern that growth follows.
  • Basic services in existing settlements. Start where density is highest. Water, sanitation and drainage connections come first, then transport links to jobs.
  • Secure tenure and housing finance. Households invest in homes they are confident they can keep. Clear land records also let cities plan and charge for services.
  • Project preparation capacity. At the 2025 Africa Investment Forum, Amadou Hott named the scarcity of bankable projects as the most severe bottleneck (AfDB). Money without prepared projects sits unspent.

Sequencing will differ by city. A secondary city with a fast-expanding edge needs the first priority more than a mature capital with a stable footprint.

Capital is scarce, so order matters. The cheapest moment to build a road is before there are houses along it.

The question for ministries and donors is not whether to invest in housing and transport. It is whether to do it now or after the backlog grows. Every year of delay adds people to settlements that will later need to be upgraded around them.
